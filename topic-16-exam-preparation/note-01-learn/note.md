# Exam preparation and synthesis

Concepts, worked examples and practical reasoning for exam preparation and synthesis.

## Build a cycle that repairs learning

Practice is most useful when it changes what you can do. Choose a skill, attempt a question without notes, mark it with the correct scheme, identify the exact gap, practise that gap and retry a different question later. Re-reading an answer can feel familiar without making independent recall easier. Keep a short error log with the question reference, the misconception or process error, the correction and a date for a delayed retry.

Use retrieval across topics. A programming trace can involve data types, Boolean logic and algorithm design at the same time. A social-impact question may require technical understanding of networks, data collection or AI. Review connections rather than isolating each definition. Explain solutions aloud, draw diagrams from memory and write short programs without copying. A quiz score is feedback about particular questions, not a complete prediction of an examination grade.

## A worked timing plan for one actual paper

The **2025 Higher Level** A/B booklet gives 90 minutes and 130 marks: Section A contributes 54 marks and Section B 76. One original practice plan is 5 minutes reading and selecting, 32 minutes for A, 45 minutes for the two B questions and 8 minutes checking. That totals 90. Allocate roughly 22–23 minutes to each selected B question, but use the subpart marks and your progress to decide when to move on. This is a suggested study strategy, not an official required division.

Its Section C paper gives 60 minutes and 80 marks. A practice plan is 5 minutes inspecting requirements and files, 40 minutes implementing in small working steps, 10 minutes testing and 5 minutes checking names and saved output. Save throughout; the last five minutes is a verification window, not the first save. Read the actual paper's permitted materials and submission instructions separately. The timetable above is specifically anchored to the 2025 Higher Level PDFs in the archive.

## Command words tell you what evidence to provide

“State” usually needs a concise fact or result. “Describe” asks what something is like or how it operates. “Explain” needs reasons or a mechanism. “Compare” identifies relevant similarities and differences under the requested headings. “Evaluate” weighs evidence, strengths, limitations and a justified judgement. “Justify” connects a choice to reasons. “Trace” follows execution in order; “calculate” shows the appropriate working; “design” proposes a solution meeting constraints.

Read the whole subpart before answering. If asked for two reasons, give two distinct reasons rather than the same point reworded. Relate an explanation to the scenario. For a comparison, use parallel criteria: memory usage against memory usage, or latency against latency. A list of unrelated facts can miss the question despite being true. Use technical terms accurately, then make their meaning clear through the example.

## Written reasoning and diagrams

For a trace, track variable values after each statement and distinguish assignment from equality. Record loop bounds and whether the final endpoint is included. For binary or hexadecimal conversions, show place values or division steps and respect the requested width. For logic, build every input combination systematically and evaluate intermediate expressions. For hardware or network questions, label components and show direction of information flow.

In data questions, show sorted values before a median and explain missing-data decisions. In modelling, state assumptions and distinguish verification from validation. In societal questions, connect a technical mechanism to a stakeholder consequence, then discuss limits or mitigation. Keep answers focused on the marks and the command word. A long paragraph does not automatically contain more creditworthy points than a clear short explanation.

## Practical programming under time

Inspect supplied code before modifying it. Identify inputs, outputs, initial values, repeated operations and required file names. Make a working copy using the requested naming convention. Implement one requirement at a time and run it immediately. Use a trace or small test to locate a failure rather than changing several unrelated lines at once. Preserve working code and save frequently.

Better Exams provides the listed PDF documents; it does **not** provide all official Section C starter files such as `Question16_A.py` or `Question16_B.py`. For practice, obtain those files through your teacher where available, or carefully recreate the code printed in the PDF, preserving names and indentation. Our `code/` examples are original teaching programs, not SEC-supplied starter files. Text extraction can lose whitespace, so check the actual PDF when reconstructing Python.

Plan normal, boundary and invalid cases. Median tasks need empty, odd and even lists; a longest-run task needs a reset after a break and a run ending at the last item. Printed spacing and formatting may be part of the required output. A program that produces a plausible result on one sample is not enough. When working from a historical scheme, inspect which parts earn marks and how partial work is treated; do not invent a universal rule.

## Self-mark with the matching scheme

Attempt before opening solutions. Mark each subpart independently and annotate the evidence in your answer. Separate a missing fact, a wrong concept, a calculation error, an incomplete explanation and a time-management issue: these need different repairs. Marking schemes can include indicative answers and accept appropriate alternatives, so use educator judgement rather than treating wording as a password. If unsure, flag the answer for teacher discussion instead of awarding yourself unsupported certainty.

Record the raw marks available and attempted. Do not mix schemes across levels or use our formative rubric as though it were an SEC allocation. For practical work, keep both the program and tests. If you repair the program after marking, retain the first attempt too; that distinction shows what you could do independently under the conditions.

## A progression through the archive

Use early attempts by topic, then combine topics, then attempt whole sections under time. The practical contexts offer variety: 2020 Higher Level password scoring; 2021 Higher Level anagrams and Ordinary Level ATM work; 2022 Higher Level dice statistics and Ordinary Level shop calculations; 2023 Higher Level guessing-game work and Ordinary Level times tables/temperature; 2024 Higher Level random-fruit work and Ordinary Level eligibility/bill splitting; 2025 Higher Level grade statistics and Ordinary Level tiling costs. These are topic pointers, not replacement questions or solutions.

Alternate levels appropriately with your teacher: a structured Ordinary Level task can strengthen a shared foundation, while a Higher Level extension introduces additional reasoning. Do not mistake a difficult context for an entirely new concept. Identify the familiar operations beneath it: validate, select, iterate, count, compare, accumulate, sort and explain.
